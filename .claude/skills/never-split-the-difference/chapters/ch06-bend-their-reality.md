# Chapter 6: Bend Their Reality

## Core Idea
Negotiation is not linear and not rational: people decide by emotion and by predictable patterns (loss aversion, anchoring, fairness). Know the hidden needs under the surface and you can reshape what the counterpart thinks they deserve. Never split the difference; use time, fairness, anchors, ranges, odd numbers and non-monetary terms to move their perception of value.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Never split the difference / No deal is better than a bad deal**: Formulation: compromise is easy and saves face, not good. When to use: whenever the pull is to "meet in the middle." How: stay curious about their real drivers, keep rapport, and walk away or keep working rather than average two positions.
- **Deadlines**: Formulation: deadlines are mostly imaginary and flexible; they make you rush and concede. When to use: any time pressure appears, yours or theirs. How: (1) don't treat your own deadline as sacred; (2) find their self-imposed deadline (month-end, quarter-end, Friday party money) and let pressure build on them; (3) reveal your deadline when it helps, since hiding it makes you negotiate against yourself and raises impasse risk; (4) read threat specificity (what, who, when, how) to gauge how near a real deadline is.
- **The F-word ("Fair")**: Formulation: fairness triggers strong emotion. Three uses. When to use: defensively and proactively. How: (1) vs. "We just want what's fair": apologize and invite fixing it, don't concede; (2) vs. "We've given you a fair offer": mirror "Fair?" then label, asking for the evidence; (3) positive: early say, "I want you to feel you're treated fairly at all times; stop me if I'm not."
- **Loss aversion (Prospect Theory)**: Formulation: people take more risk to avoid a loss than to gain. When to use: whenever you need action. How: show the counterpart something concrete they lose by inaction, not just what you can deliver.
- **Anchor their emotions**: Formulation: run an accusation audit before the bad news so the real offer feels like relief. When to use: before delivering a low offer or bad news.
- **Let the other guy go first (mostly)**: When to use: when you lack market information. How: prepare to withstand an extreme first number; open yourself only against rookies or equally informed pros, remembering your reputation.
- **Establish a range**: How: allude to a comparable deal range whose low end is what you actually want (a "bolstering range"); expect them to land at the low end.
- **Pivot to nonmonetary terms**: How: after anchoring, trade items cheap for them and valuable to you, or the reverse; throw out examples to start brainstorming.
- **Use odd numbers**: Round numbers feel like placeholders; precise ones feel calculated and firm.
- **Surprise with a gift**: How: after an extreme anchor and their rejection, give an unrelated gift to trigger reciprocity.

## Key Concepts
- **Win-win trap**: Splitting the difference often leaves both sides worse off, like one black shoe and one brown.
- **Ultimatum Game**: Shows no shared notion of "fair"; people reject free money to punish insult.
- **Projection vs. empathy**: Assuming the other thinks like you is projection.
- **Certainty Effect**: Sure things are preferred over better gambles.
- **Anchor and adjustment**: Estimates drift from the first number heard.
- **Accusation audit**: Naming all their negative thoughts about you before they do.
- **Winner's curse / buyer's remorse**: Regret from overpaying or underselling.
- **Reciprocity**: People feel obliged to repay kindness.
- **Pleasant persistence**: Smiling, repeated asks that act as emotional anchors.

## Mental Models
- Use time pressure on them when you can find their real clock; ignore your own.
- Use an accusation audit plus loss aversion when you must deliver a bad price: "You'll think I'm a lousy businessman... but I wanted to offer it to you before someone else."
- Use a range rather than a number when you must name terms first.
- Use a precise odd number (e.g. $4,751) when you want an offer to read as final.
- Use "Fair?" mirrored back when someone weaponizes fairness.

## Anti-patterns
- Splitting the difference to feel reasonable.
- Hiding your deadline and then conceding faster as it nears.
- Opening first with a number when you don't know the market.
- Using round numbers to show a firm limit.
- Sounding rational about fairness instead of treating it as emotion.
- Giving a salary number without success terms, metrics, or a boss with a stake in you.

## Worked Example
Haitian kidnappers demanded $150,000 for an aunt. Debrief revealed they wanted party money by Friday, so the FBI stalled. Coached lines: "How are we supposed to pay if you're going to hurt her?" and "How can I come up with that kind of money?" pushed their demand from $150k to $50k to $25k without the family naming a price. Then the nephew gave an extreme low anchor of $3,000, followed by the odd $4,751 after they dropped to $10,000 then $7,500, with an extra gift of a CD stereo. They accepted and the aunt came home safely.

## Key Takeaways
- Never split the difference; no deal beats a bad deal.
- Deadlines are mostly artificial; the pressure is on the side that believes in them.
- "Fair" is an emotional lever; respond with mirroring and labeling, and use it proactively.
- Anchor emotions first, numbers later; use ranges, odd numbers and non-monetary terms.
- Make them see a loss from inaction.
- For salary: be pleasantly persistent on non-salary terms, define success metrics, and ask "What does it take to be successful here?"
