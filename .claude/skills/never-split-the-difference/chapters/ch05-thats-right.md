# Chapter 5: Trigger the Two Words That Immediately Transform Any Negotiation

## Core Idea
"Yes" is cheap and often a brush-off; "That's right" is the real breakthrough. It signals the counterpart feels fully understood and has agreed, of their own free will, with your account of their world. You get there with a Summary: paraphrase plus label. Once unconditional positive regard exists, behavior can actually change. The chapter rests on the Behavioral Change Stairway Model: active listening, empathy, rapport, influence, behavioral change.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Behavioral Change Stairway Model (BCSM)**: Five stages, in order: active listening, empathy, rapport, influence, behavioral change. When to use: any time you want lasting change rather than momentary compliance. How: climb the steps in order; do not jump to influence before rapport.
- **Summary (paraphrase + label)**: Formulation: Summary = rearticulating the meaning of what they said + acknowledging the emotion underneath. When to use: when the counterpart is stuck, hostile, or repeating a fixed position. How: (1) let them talk and drain the emotion using effective pauses and minimal encouragers; (2) mirror; (3) label; (4) paraphrase in your own words; (5) deliver "the world according to X" fully; (6) stay silent until they say "That's right."
- **Active listening toolkit**: Effective pauses, minimal encouragers ("Yes," "OK," "I see"), mirroring, labeling, paraphrasing, summarizing. When to use: as the build-up that makes a summary land.
- **"That's right" vs. "You're right" test**: When to use: to judge whether you've really connected. How: "That's right" means they embraced the point; "You're right" means they want you to stop talking.

## Key Concepts
- **Unconditional positive regard**: Accepting someone as they are, which lowers their need to hide what they think.
- **That's right**: A realization point where the counterpart agrees without feeling they've given in.
- **You're right**: A dismissal that signals no ownership and no behavior change.
- **Subtle epiphany**: The counterpart doesn't notice the shift; they just embrace what you said.
- **Summary**: Paraphrase plus label, the trigger for "That's right."
- **Black Swans (preview)**: Hidden underlying drivers that often surface right after "That's right."
- **Yes is nothing without how**: Commitment needs a path to execution, not a polite agreement.
- **The world according to...**: Framing for the full restatement of the other side's reality.

## Mental Models
- Use a Summary when the other side is dug in and logic, questions and reasoning bounce off.
- Use a label on your own ally when they resist your strategy: name the feeling first, then negotiate.
- Use the "That's right" test to separate real agreement from polite agreement before you build on it.
- Use a summary to probe a stonewalling counterpart for motive; after it, ask "Is there any other help you need?" to invite the full story.
- Use "That's right" in price talks to reveal their real motivation before accepting a split-the-difference number.

## Anti-patterns
- Settling for "Yes" or "You're right" and treating it as buy-in.
- Pitching your product or point before the counterpart feels understood.
- Arguing facts with someone driven by emotion (e.g. countering "war damages" with logic).
- Summarizing without the emotional label, which turns it into parroting.
- Rushing the summary or forcing "That's right" early in the conversation.

## Worked Example
Pharma rep, skeptical doctor. In early visits he interrupts her pitch and dismisses her product. Next visit she says nothing about the product: "Last time we talked, you seemed passionate about treating these patients and tailoring treatment to each one." He answers, "That's right," and explains his view that he is treating an epidemic others miss. She lets him describe his difficult cases, then summarizes: "You tailor specific treatments for each patient." "That's right." Only then does she position her medication against the outcomes he has just named. He says, "Let me give yours a try."

## Key Takeaways
- Aim for "That's right," not "Yes."
- Summary = label + paraphrase, delivered after pauses and mirroring have drained emotion.
- "You're right" is a polite way to end the conversation; don't mistake it for agreement.
- "That's right" is a stealth victory: the counterpart owns the conclusion.
- Right after it, ask open questions; hidden motives (Black Swans) tend to surface.
- Use it on allies and family too, not only adversaries.
