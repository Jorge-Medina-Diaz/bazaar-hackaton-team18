# Chapter 8: Guarantee Execution

## Core Idea
A "Yes" is worthless without a "How." Your job is not to reach agreement but to reach one that gets implemented, so you act as a decision architect: you shape the verbal and nonverbal environment to win consent from the table, from the players behind it, and from the counterpart's own follow-through. Calibrated "How" questions make the counterpart design and own the solution, expose fake agreement, and politely say "No" while the other side keeps bidding against itself.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **"How am I supposed to do that?" (forced empathy)**: A deferential "How" question is a gentle No that invites the counterpart to solve your problem. When to use: any demand you cannot or will not meet, especially after you have shown empathy. How: (1) deliver with a request-for-help tone, not an accusation; (2) stay silent and let them rework the offer; (3) repeat with new "How/What" variants.
- **Four-step "No" series (bid against themselves)**: You can say No four times before saying the word. How: (1) "How am I supposed to do that?"; (2) "Your offer is very generous, I'm sorry, that just doesn't work for me"; (3) "I'm sorry, I'm afraid I just can't do that"; (4) "I'm sorry, no"; only then a gentle, downward-inflected "No." Make no counteroffer in between.
- **Behind-the-table (Level II) analysis**: Deal killers matter more than deal makers. When to use: any committee, boss, or advisor decision. How: ask "How does this affect the rest of your team?", "How on board are the people not on this call?", "What do your colleagues see as their main challenges?"
- **Defining success in their words**: Ask "How will we know we're on track?" and "How will we address things if we find we're off track?"; summarize their answers until you get "That's right."
- **7-38-55 Percent Rule**: 7% words, 38% tone, 55% body language. Watch for incongruence and use a label ("I heard you say Yes, but there was hesitation in your voice").
- **Rule of Three**: Get the same agreement three times in one conversation, varying the method (commitment, then a label/summary, then an implementation "How/What" question; or one question phrased three ways).
- **Pinocchio Effect / pronoun reading**: Liars use more words, complex sentences, and more third-person pronouns. Frequent I/me/my signals low authority; I-avoidant we/they language signals a real decision maker.
- **Chris Discount**: Introduce yourself by first name ("My name is Chris. What's the Chris discount?") to humanize yourself and trigger forced empathy, instead of hammering their name.

## Key Concepts
- **Decision architect**: Designing the interaction so agreement is both given and carried out.
- **Three kinds of Yes**: Commitment (real), Confirmation (a reflexive factual yes), Counterfeit (a dodge to escape pressure).
- **Forced empathy**: A question that makes the counterpart examine your situation and feel obliged to help.
- **Illusion of control**: Answering questions makes them feel in charge while you steer.
- **Level II players**: Parties not at the table who can enable or block the deal.
- **"I'll try"**: Really means "I plan to fail"; "You're right" signals no buy-in.
- **Incongruence**: Words that do not match tone or body language.
- **Bidding against themselves**: A counterpart treats your soft No as a counterbid and lowers their own ask.

## Mental Models
- Use a calibrated "How" when you want to refuse without confrontation or stall for time.
- Use the Rule of Three when a Yes sounds too smooth or the stakes of implementation are high.
- Use deference with jerks: absorb aggression like tai chi and answer with What/How instead of counteroffers.
- Use label-the-incongruence when tone contradicts words, before the agreement hardens.
- Use ownership: people execute harder when the solution feels like their idea.

## Anti-patterns
- Accepting a Yes (or "You're right" or "I'll try") without testing it.
- Assuming the people on the call are the only decision makers.
- Jumping in with your full offer and calling it "very fair," which implies disagreement is unfair.
- Mentally leaving the closing (flight times, golf) before the last "No" step is done.
- Going chin to chin with an aggressive counterpart.
- Hammering their name like a slick salesman.

## Worked Example
Joaquin wanted to buy out his partner Bruno cheaply. After mirroring and an accusation audit, Bruno revealed a bank loan squeeze. Joaquin then blurted a full offer ("very fair"), and Bruno retreated behind an advisor demanding a precise 30,812 euros. Joaquin and his brother Jesus used the No series: No. 1 was a warm message with no counter ("I wish I could afford it") and the price dropped to 28,346; No. 2 cited his divorce and cash limits and the price dropped to 25,000; No. 3 said even his mother would not lend, and offered 23,567 with a precise 15,321.37 up front and the rest over a year. The advisor accepted within the hour.

## Key Takeaways
- "Yes" is nothing without "How"; get them to explain implementation in their own words.
- A "How can I do that?" is a No that makes them bid against themselves.
- Map and influence the people behind the table early.
- Test every agreement with the Rule of Three.
- Tone, body language, word count, and pronouns reveal lies and real authority.
- Humanize yourself (the Chris Discount) and stay deferential to jerks.
