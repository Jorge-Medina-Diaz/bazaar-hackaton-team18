# Chapter 9: Bargain Hard

## Core Idea
Bargaining is where most people wimp out, yet it is a psychological game, not just arithmetic. Know your own and your counterpart's negotiating style (Analyst, Accommodator, Assertive), learn to absorb an extreme anchor without folding, use assertion deliberately and without enmity, and run the Ackerman model so that a hard price haggle ends on your target while the counterpart feels they squeezed every last drop.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three negotiating styles (Analyst / Accommodator / Assertive)**: Treat others the way they need to be treated (the "Black Swan rule"), not the Golden Rule. When to use: before bargaining, to tune tactics and read silence.
  - **Analyst**: methodical, time is for getting it right, hates surprises, sensitive to reciprocity, cold flat voice. Tactics: bring data, no ad-libbing, warn early, avoid surprises, do not fire questions at them, let silence be thinking time, expect delayed answers; if you are one, smile when you speak.
  - **Accommodator**: relationship first, talks a lot, concedes to appease, may promise what they cannot deliver, hides objections to avoid conflict. Tactics: be friendly, use calibrated questions about implementation, probe for unspoken objections; if you are one, keep likability but voice objections and cut chitchat.
  - **Assertive**: time is money, wants to be heard first, tells rather than asks, "give an inch, take a mile" on reciprocity. Tactics: mirrors, labels, summaries, get the "That's right"; if you are one, soften your tone.
- **Taking a punch (deflecting an extreme anchor)**: When to use: they open with a ridiculous number. How: (1) say No indirectly ("How am I supposed to accept that?"); (2) deflect with "What are we trying to accomplish here?"; (3) pivot to terms ("Let's put price aside and talk about what would make this a good deal"; "What else could you offer to make that a good price for me?"); (4) if pushed to go first, cite a high third-party number; (5) prefer they anchor first so you see their hand.
- **Punching back**: Strategic umbrage (take real, controlled offense at the proposal, not the person: "I don't see how that would ever work"), threats without anger delivered with poise, defensive "Why" questions that make them argue your side ("Why would you ever change from your supplier? They're great!"), "I" messages ("I'm sorry, that doesn't work for me"; "I feel ___ when you ___ because ___"), and a ready-to-walk mindset.
- **Ackerman model**: Offer-counteroffer system that uses an extreme anchor and shrinking raises. Steps: (1) set your target price (your goal); (2) set the first offer at 65% of target; (3) plan three raises of decreasing size to 85%, 95%, and 100% of target (raises of roughly 20, 10, 5 points); (4) before each raise, use empathy, calibrated questions, and varied No's to make them counter first; (5) make the final amount a precise, nonround number (for example 37,893 rather than 38,000); (6) on the final number add a nonmonetary item they probably do not want, to signal you are at your limit.
- **Time-out de-escalation**: Punching back is a last resort; suggest a time-out and return to rapport.

## Key Concepts
- **Extreme anchor**: A shocking opening number that triggers fight-or-flight and pushes them toward their limit.
- **ZOPA**: Zone of Possible Agreement; rational theory that experienced bargainers ignore in practice.
- **Compromise trap**: Drifting toward meeting in the middle.
- **"I am normal" paradox**: Assuming others see the world as you do, so you miss their style (about two in three counterparts differ).
- **Reciprocity**: Concessions invite concessions; the shrinking raises exploit it.
- **Pivot to terms**: Moving from price to nonmonetary conditions.
- **Strategic umbrage**: Deliberate offense-taking; know it so it is not used on you.
- **Silence meanings**: Analyst thinking, Accommodator anger, Assertive "nothing to say" or "you talk."
- **Nonround number**: A precise figure that feels calculated and credible.

## Mental Models
- Use mirrors and summaries on Assertives, data on Analysts, relationship and implementation questions on Accommodators.
- Use Ackerman when you face a hard haggler and need to beat the meet-in-the-middle outcome.
- Use "pivot to terms" when pulled into a price haggle.
- Treat any response that is not an outright rejection as an edge for you.
- Use the person-versus-problem frame: the counterpart is never the enemy, the unsolved issue is.

## Anti-patterns
- Naming a price first when you could let them anchor.
- Treating your counterpart's style as your own.
- Folding to your maximum at the first punch.
- Faking anger (it backfires) or letting real anger run uncontrolled.
- Using "Why?" casually.
- Raising your offer without making them counter first (improvising two moves at once is fine only when you feel in control).
- Needing the deal so badly you cannot say No.
- Making round-number final offers.

## Worked Example
Mishary's lease renewal, with a 2,100/month ask and his goal of 1,830: he opened with an anchor of 1,730 and asked "How do you price renewals?" to gather data. He raised to 1,790. The agent countered at 1,950. Mishary replied "generous, but how am I supposed to accept that," then raised to 1,810, then did visible pen-and-paper calculations and gave a final precise 1,829. The agent accepted for twelve months. In the 4Runner purchase, Voss opened at 30,000 cash against a 36,000 sticker and used only "How am I supposed to do that?" and "generous... I just can't" until the dealer reached 30,000.

## Key Takeaways
- Identify style first; the Golden Rule is wrong at the table.
- Prepare: you fall to your highest level of preparation.
- Welcome their anchor, deflect it with How/What questions or a pivot to terms.
- Ackerman: 65, 85, 95, 100 percent, decreasing raises, precise final number, and a nonmonetary add-on.
- Assert with calibrated, controlled force and keep the relationship intact.
- Be ready to walk away; no deal beats a bad deal.
