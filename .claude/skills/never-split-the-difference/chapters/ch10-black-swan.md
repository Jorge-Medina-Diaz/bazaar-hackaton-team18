# Chapter 10: Find the Black Swan

## Core Idea
Every negotiation hides unknown unknowns, "Black Swans," pieces of information that change the game when found. Voss hypothesizes each side holds at least three. Finding them requires dropping your known-knowns template, treating "crazy" behavior as a clue, listening for the counterpart's "religion" (worldview), seeking face time and unguarded moments, and recognizing three kinds of leverage: positive, negative, and normative.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Known knowns / known unknowns / unknown unknowns**: Let what you know guide, not blind you; each case is new. How: keep a beginner's mind and ask "Why are they communicating this right now?"
- **Three types of leverage**: Leverage is the counterpart's perception of what they stand to lose or gain; it can be manufactured.
  - **Positive**: your ability to give or withhold what they want (delay offers, create competition, find their other wants).
  - **Negative**: ability to make them suffer (reputation, status); use sparingly, never as a threat; labeling it ("It seems you value always paying on time") is subtler.
  - **Normative**: using their own rules and norms; expose inconsistency between their beliefs and actions; speak their language.
- **Know their religion**: Understand the worldview that governs their decisions and frame your proposal inside it. Tips: review everything you hear, compare notes with teammates, use backup listeners who only listen between the lines.
- **The Similarity Principle**: People trust and concede to those who seem similar; surface shared identity (for example "This is really stewardship for you, isn't it?").
- **Hopes and dreams**: Express passion for their unattained goals and their ability to reach them, so you become someone they follow.
- **Religion as a reason**: A "because" reason boosts compliance (Langer's copier study: 60% rising to over 90%); better still, a reason that references their religion.
- **"It's not crazy, it's a clue" (three mistakes)**: When they seem irrational, check whether they are (1) ill-informed (supply information), (2) constrained (legal, promises, precedent, lack of authority), or (3) pursuing other interests (status, fairness, timing, hidden goals).
- **Finding Black Swans**: Get face time; observe unguarded moments (start and end of meetings, interruptions, meals); when something does not make sense, use labels (even a deliberate mislabel invites correction).

## Key Concepts
- **Black Swan**: Unknown unknown that is a leverage multiplier.
- **Leverage as emotional**: What counts is what they believe you can do to them.
- **Paradox of power**: The harder you push, the more resistance you meet.
- **Backup listener**: A teammate dedicated to hearing subtext.
- **Face time over email**: Email lets people re-center and hides tone and nonverbals.
- **Constraint**: Hidden limit that makes a sane counterpart look irrational.
- **GIGO**: Garbage in, garbage out; bad information produces bad choices.
- **Fear of conflict**: The amygdala urges you to give up or lash out; the adversary is the situation.

## Mental Models
- Use "They're crazy" as a trigger to look for constraints, bad information, or hidden interests.
- Use face time when email feels guarded; a dinner with no business talk can unlock admissions.
- Use normative leverage when you can quote their stated standards back to them.
- Use labels and calibrated "What/How" questions, prepared in advance, so you can pounce when a Black Swan appears.
- Use patience: focus on the next step on the tightrope, not the end objective.

## Anti-patterns
- Forcing new situations into old templates (the FBI at the Rochester bank).
- Writing off a counterpart as crazy or "a terrorist" and refusing to engage.
- Threatening directly or shoving negative leverage down their throat.
- Relying on email and formal sessions only.
- Verifying what you expect instead of hearing what is there.
- Avoiding conflict to be liked.

## Worked Example
A Georgetown student asked a Charleston broker calibrated questions about the market and labeled his expertise ("sounds like I'm in good hands"). Told the property was a 100% occupied cash cow, he mislabeled the seller ("must have doubts about future fundamentals"), and the broker corrected him: the seller had troubled properties elsewhere and needed cash fast. With that constraint, the student anchored at 3.4 million against a 4.3 million ask, got a 3.7 million counter, answered 3.55, and closed at 3.6 million. The Dwight Watson standoff followed the same logic: listening showed he was a veteran with rules and a devout Christian, so the team told him tomorrow was the "Dawn of the Third Day," and he surrendered.

## Key Takeaways
- Assume each side holds at least three Black Swans and hunt for them.
- Leverage comes in positive, negative, and normative forms; use negative sparingly.
- Learn their worldview and speak within it.
- "Crazy" usually means ill-informed, constrained, or driven by other interests.
- Get face time and watch unguarded moments.
- Prepare labels and questions in advance so you can act on the surprise.
- Embrace honest conflict; the adversary is the situation, not the person.
