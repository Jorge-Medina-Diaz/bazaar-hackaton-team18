# Appendix: Prepare a Negotiation One Sheet

## Core Idea
Negotiation is a psychological investigation, and under pressure you fall to your highest level of preparation. A one-page "one sheet" lists the tools you plan to use, customized to the deal. It is not a rigid script; it keeps you agile and covers goal, facts, accusations, questions, and noncash value. Voss claims good preparation returns at least 7:1 in time saved on renegotiating and clarifying implementation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Negotiation One Sheet (five sections)**: When to use: before every negotiation, from a big deal to a rent talk. How: fill the five sections below on a single page, rehearse aloud, carry it in.
  - **I. The Goal**: Think through best and worst cases, but write down only one specific, optimistic-but-reasonable best-case goal. Four steps: set it and define it clearly; write it down; discuss it with a colleague to prevent wimping out; carry the written goal into the room.
  - **II. Summary**: A couple of sentences of known facts that led to the negotiation (why you are there, what you want, what they want, why), phrased so the counterpart would say "That's right."
  - **III. Labels / Accusation Audit**: List the accusations they might make, however unfair, and turn each into labels (three to five in total, at most five per accusation); role-play them. Fill-in-the-blank labels: "It seems like ___ is valuable to you," "It seems like you don't like ___," "It seems like you value ___," "It seems like ___ makes it easier," "It seems like you're reluctant to ___."
  - **IV. Calibrated Questions**: Prepare three to five "What/How" questions that reveal value and expose deal killers. Core set: "What are we trying to accomplish?", "How is that worthwhile?", "What's the core issue here?", "How does that affect things?", "What's the biggest challenge you face?", "How does this fit into what the objective is?" Behind-the-table: "How does this affect the rest of your team?", "How on board are the people not on this call?" Deal-killer questions: "What are we up against here?", "What happens if you do nothing?", "What does doing nothing cost you?", "How does making this deal resonate with what your company prides itself on?" Ask in groups of two or three. Follow-up labels: "It seems like ___ is important," "It seems you feel like my company is in a unique position to ___," "It seems like you are worried that ___."
  - **V. Noncash Offers**: List noncash items they hold that would be valuable, asking "What could they give that would almost get us to do it for free?" (for example a magazine cover story instead of cash).

## Key Concepts
- **BATNA**: Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement (Fisher and Ury); Voss argues it becomes your target and caps your ask.
- **Wimp-win**: Aiming low to protect self-esteem and then settling at your bottom line.
- **High-end goal**: A specific, challenging, realistic target that primes you to feel losses below it.
- **Position vs. motivation**: Look past stated demands to worries and hopes.
- **Behind-the-table deal killers**: People comfortable with the status quo who lose face from change.
- **Perceived losses**: A loss stings at least twice as much as an equal gain.
- **Nonfinancial needs**: Self-esteem, status, autonomy, often decisive.
- **Scripted preparation**: The trap of preordaining the exact flow of the negotiation.

## Mental Models
- Use a high-end written goal, not a bottom line, as your focal point.
- Use noncash trades when the counterpart wants to look good rather than save money.
- Use two or three similar questions together to see the same issue from several angles.
- Use prepared labels to turn their answers back and keep information flowing.
- Use role-play of accusations to remove fear before entering the room.

## Anti-patterns
- Making BATNA your centerpiece.
- Preparing only bottom line, wants, and counterarguments, which yields offer, counteroffer, split the difference.
- Writing a full script and losing agility.
- Focusing only on money when the real blocker is self-esteem or a colleague.
- Assuming the other side's needs necessarily conflict with yours.
- Skipping preparation and trusting you will rise to the occasion.

## Worked Example
You sell used speakers and want 100 dollars. A bottom-line focus relaxes at 100. Knowing used stores list them at 140, you set a written goal of 150 and stay open to more. Another case: a landlord opposes subletters, so the prepared labels are "It seems as though you're not a fan of subletters" and "It seems like you want stability with your tenants." And a seller of an accounting system faces a buyer afraid of errors before his annual review; instead of cutting price, promise installation in 90 days, guaranteed.

## Key Takeaways
- Put five sections on one page: Goal, Summary, Labels/Accusation Audit, Calibrated Questions, Noncash Offers.
- Write only the best-case goal; people who expect more and say it get more.
- Prepare 3 to 5 labels and 3 to 5 calibrated questions.
- Find noncash value they can cheaply give.
- Prepare to adapt, not to script.
